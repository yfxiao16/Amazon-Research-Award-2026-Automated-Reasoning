\section{Methods}

The core of \sysname is a neurosymbolic loop: LLM agents propose decompositions (Thrust~2), a contract engine certifies them on the theory of Thrust~1, and its verdicts return to the agents in context and as post-training rewards (Thrust~3). We will evaluate it on distributed systems, multi-robot control, and hardware.

\thrust{Thrust 1: Contract Theory for Decomposition.} Timing contracts for distributed systems associate the logical ordering of events with execution and communication latencies, and bound the inconsistency and unavailability of each component~\cite{xiao2026memocode}. Robot contracts couple a GR(1) mission layer~\cite{bloem2012gr1} with controllers certified by control barrier functions~\cite{ames2019cbf} or reachability; learned policies, such as \gls{vla} models~\cite{kim2024openvla}, are guarded at run time by safety filters and monitors that report violated assumptions to the decomposition, and are post-trained with rewards derived from their component contracts. Hardware contracts relate the ports of register-transfer-level modules over clock cycles and may refer to ghost state verified on the design. Since adjacent levels often use different formalisms, such as a GR(1) mission refined by controllers certified by barrier functions, we will relate their contracts through embeddings based on conservative approximations~\cite{naik2024contract}, under which a refinement established between embedded contracts implies refinement across formalisms, so that every step of a heterogeneous decomposition can be certified by an existing tool.

\thrust{Thrust 2: Agentic Decomposition Search.} \sysname represents a design as a directed acyclic graph of contracts, built recursively by LLM agents. At an internal node, an agent receives the parent contract, the interfaces of the subcomponents, and certified decompositions of similar components retrieved from the library, and proposes one contract for each subcomponent, formalizing natural-language requirements where needed. The proposal is accepted only if the component contracts are consistent and non-vacuous, bounded checks on the actual components find no counterexample, and their composition refines the parent contract. Each subcomponent is then discharged by a back end or handed to a new agent whose context contains only its own contract and therefore does not grow with the design; a node that cannot satisfy its contract returns a witness to its parent, which revises the decomposition while certified subtrees are kept. Alternative decompositions are explored by a tree search in which the LLM acts as the policy and the engine prunes candidates. Only the leaves change across domains: a networked component whose timing contract is checked via mixed-integer linear programming, a robot controller certified by a synthesis or reachability tool, a learned policy guarded at run time, or a hardware module proved by a model checker.

\thrust{Thrust 3: Learning from Formal Feedback.} The engine separates a formalism-independent contract algebra, checks reduced to validity or satisfiability, and formalism-specific analyses such as unrealizable cores, vacuity~\cite{maoz2020vacuity}, and well-separation~\cite{maoz2016wellsep}; every failed check returns a counterexample or a minimal core that points to the responsible terms. A \gls{mcp} server and an agent skill expose these operations to agents, while no LLM is part of the engine. On a failed check, the counterexample is returned to the agent in context as a trace over the responsible contract terms. As learned clauses prune the search of a SAT solver, the core of a failed decomposition is generalized via subgraph isomorphism~\cite{xiao2024contrarc} into a constraint that excludes every candidate assigning an isomorphic component the same terms. Certified contracts are stored with their components in a library that grows with each design, and whose coverage is measured by injecting faults. Beyond in-context feedback, certified decompositions, together with the module hierarchies of existing designs as weak supervision, are used for supervised fine-tuning of an open-weight proposer; reinforcement learning then uses the checks of the engine as verifiable rewards, graded by the consistency, refinement, and realizability of each component contract, so that partially correct decompositions still provide signal. As the engine checks every proposal, soundness never depends on the proposer.

\thrust{Evaluation and preliminary results.} Our prior work on auto-formalization~\cite{ni2026agentified,ni2026draftprune} improves its reliability by screening diverse candidate formalizations with solvers, a principle that \sysname applies to contract generation and revision. For distributed systems, our timing contracts~\cite{xiao2026memocode} verified and repaired the consistency and availability requirements of an automatic emergency braking system and a multi-drone coordination scenario. In multi-robot control, ACIES~\cite{xiao2026acies} decomposes mission requirements into GR(1) contracts, synthesizes controllers, and executes them under control barrier functions with runtime monitors, reusing certified components from its contract library. In hardware, component contracts are \glspl{sva} with ghost-state models, discharged by hardware model checkers. Our prototype certified an end-to-end in-order delivery property of a HierSVA asynchronous link with 25 model-checking runs and about 16,000 LLM input tokens, while direct model checking of the end-to-end property did not finish within a considerably longer time limit; the decomposition found at FIFO depth 8 certified depths 16 to 512 with no further LLM calls, and an agent without the protocol used 24 times as many tokens without completing the proof. We will compare frontier models with and without formal feedback and retrieval against the post-trained proposer, measuring the requirements certified beyond direct verification or synthesis, the acceptance rate, and solver runs and tokens per certificate.
