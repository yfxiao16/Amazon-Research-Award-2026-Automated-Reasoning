\section{Introduction}

\Gls{llm} agents are increasingly employed to design complex systems, from distributed cyber-physical systems, such as an automatic emergency braking system whose networked components must trade consistency against availability~\cite{xiao2026memocode}, to multi-robot systems and custom silicon. Language models translate natural-language requirements into temporal logic~\cite{cosler2023nl2spec} and generate hardware assertions~\cite{orenes2023llmfv,kang2024fveval}. However, AI-generated designs often contain errors, and these errors concentrate in decomposition, which every complex design requires. In our experience, agents that decompose end-to-end hardware properties may report timed-out runs as proofs. Over long design tasks, agents disobey the task specification and lose track of earlier context~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Moreover, the reasoning behind a design is buried in transcripts, so a late error cannot be traced to its cause. As a result, AI-driven design relies today on trial and error and on testing the final artifact. A new generation of design methods would be essential to make every step of AI-driven design traceable and checkable, and to keep the overall design correct as it is decomposed across components and abstraction levels.

Contract-based design provides a formal basis for this goal. An \gls{ag} contract specifies what a component guarantees under assumptions on its environment~\cite{benveniste2018contracts}. A decomposition is correct when the composition of the component contracts refines the system contract, that is, when the composed components can replace the system in any environment the system contract allows~\cite{incer2023algebraic,incer2022thesis}. Existing contract-based tools, including \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}, Pacti~\cite{incer2023pacti}, and OCRA~\cite{cimatti2013ocra}, assume that contracts are written by human designers, and existing decomposition methods for synthesis rely on a fixed library of components~\cite{iannopollo2018decomposition}. Decomposition is also used by recent LLM reasoning methods: language models search over decompositions~\cite{yao2023tot}, compose functions validated by tests~\cite{zelikman2023parsel}, and delegate subgoals to provers~\cite{jiang2022thor,jiang2023dsp,ren2025deepseekprover}. However, their checks are either informal, such as tests and verbal reflection~\cite{shinn2023reflexion}, or limited to proof goals, and do not establish that the components of a system are realizable, i.e., can be implemented, and compose correctly. Letting agents write the contracts raises three scientific questions. First, the levels of a design are often described in different formalisms, so each decomposition step must remain checkable by existing verification and synthesis tools. Second, the existence of a decomposition into realizable components is undecidable for general architectures~\cite{pnueli1990distributed}, so the search must never lose certified progress. Third, an agent may search indefinitely unless each failure is explained in a form that excludes whole families of candidates.

The proposed framework, termed \sysname, addresses these challenges via a three-pronged approach. The central principle of \sysname is that agents propose and contracts certify: a step proposed by an agent is accepted only with a certificate that a solver can check, so that guarantees established at higher abstraction levels propagate to the implementation. First, \sysname extends contract theory so that decomposition in space, across abstraction levels, and over time can be checked by existing tools (Thrust~1). Second, it develops an agentic decomposition search in which LLM agents, guided by a library of certified components, propose contracts at every level (Thrust~2). Finally, it learns from formal feedback by repairing failed decompositions and growing libraries of certified contracts and repair patterns (Thrust~3). \sysname builds on our prior work on contract-based design methodologies~\cite{nuzzo2015platform} and extends the \chase framework~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}, which provides a contract algebra with interfaces to verification and synthesis tools, recently extended with an interface for LLM agents. Our work on contract-based architecture exploration~\cite{xiao2024contrarc,xiao2026smcp} has shown that infeasibility certificates can be generalized via subgraph isomorphism to prune large design spaces, and received the Best Paper Award at DATE 2024. Our timing contracts enabled consistency and availability analysis of distributed systems~\cite{xiao2026memocode}, while ACIES~\cite{xiao2026acies} and our ongoing work apply agent-proposed contracts to multi-robot control and \gls{rtl} verification. If successful, this project will lay the foundations for AI-driven design of complex systems in which every step is certified by a solver, errors are traced to their cause, and components are reused.
