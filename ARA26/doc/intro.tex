\section{Introduction}

\Gls{llm} agents are increasingly being employed to aid the design of complex engineered systems, from software and hardware systems to distributed multi-agent cyber-physical systems~\cite{xiao2026memocode} such as 
% an automatic emergency braking system whose networked components must trade consistency against availability, to 
multi-robot systems. Language models can help translate natural-language requirements into formal specifications and assertions expressed using temporal logics~\cite{cosler2023nl2spec} or hardware description languages~\cite{orenes2023llmfv,kang2024fveval}. However, existing approaches to AI-augmented design are prone to pitfalls and design errors, especially when dealing with the top-down  decomposition of system-level specifications or bottom-up integration of component implementation, which are necessary steps in a design flow. Over long design tasks, agents may even disobey their task specifications or lose track of context information available from earlier design stages~\cite{cemri2025mast}. The reasoning behind a sequence of design decisions gets often buried in a set of transcripts, which makes it difficult to trace errors found late in the design process back to their root causes. As a result, AI-human collaboration in system design relies today on trial and error and expensive tests performed on the design artifacts at each design stage. A new generation of design methods would be essential to enable traceability and verifiability in AI-augmented design, and provide strong guarantees of correctness across components and abstraction levels.

Contract-based design can provide a formal foundation toward this goal. An \gls{ag} contract specifies what a component guarantees under assumptions on its environment~\cite{benveniste2018contracts}. A decomposition is correct when the composition of the component contracts refines the system contract, that is, when any implementation of the composite contract can also implement the system contract in any valid environment. 
% ~\cite{incer2023algebraic,incer2022thesis}. 
Existing contract-based tools such as \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}
% Pacti~\cite{incer2023pacti}, and OCRA~\cite{cimatti2013ocra}, 
assume that contracts are written by human designers, while existing methods to automatically decompose specifications rely on a fixed library of components and contracts~\cite{iannopollo2018decomposition}. Decomposition approaches have also been recently pursued by relying on LLM reasoning capabilities: language models can search over a space of possible goal decompositions~\cite{yao2023tot}, compose tasks, validate them by tests~\cite{zelikman2023parsel}, and delegate subgoals to various provers~\cite{jiang2022thor,jiang2023dsp,ren2025deepseekprover}. However, in existing LLM-based frameworks, verification steps are either informal, e.g., based on verbal reflection~\cite{shinn2023reflexion} and a limited number of tests, or they are limited to a single abstraction level in the design flow and a single formalism.

Delegating to agents the task of writing contracts raises two challenges. First, specifications can be decomposed over different domains, e.g., in space, over time, and across levels of abstraction that use different formalisms, making it difficult to appropriately orchestrate different tools to ensure traceability. 
% and each step must remain checkable by existing tools. 
Second, the problem of decomposing temporal tasks into subtasks that are realizable is undecidable, in general, and often intractable, in practice, even if the system architecture, i.e., the system components and their interconnections, is given~\cite{pnueli1990distributed}. It is therefore desirable to devise mechanisms that can accelerate the search and prune the design space by leveraging all the certified invariants and the counterexamples found via verification methods throughout the design process. 
% must never lose certified progress.

The proposed framework, termed \sysname, addresses these challenges via a three-pronged approach. The central principle of \sysname is that agents propose and contracts certify: a step proposed by an agent is accepted only when a solver certifies it, so that guarantees established at higher abstraction levels propagate to every certified implementation. First, \sysname develops representations of the design as hierarchical contract networks\yifeng{~\cite{naik2024contract,wang2022hierarchical}} that combine decomposition in space, across abstraction levels, and over time, so that every step can be checked by existing tools (Thrust~1). Second, it develops an agentic decomposition search in which LLM agents, guided by a library of certified contracts, propose contracts at every level (Thrust~2). Finally, agents learn from verification outcomes by using
diagnoses to repair failed decompositions and retaining certified
contracts and reusable repair patterns (Thrust~3). \sysname builds on our prior work on contract-based design methodologies~\cite{nuzzo2015platform} and on the \chase \yifeng{(Contract-based Heterogeneous Analysis and System Exploration)} framework~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}, which connects a contract algebra to verification and synthesis tools and, recently, to LLM agents. Our work on contract-based architecture exploration~\cite{xiao2024contrarc,xiao2026smcp} has shown that infeasibility certificates can prune large design spaces, and their generalization via subgraph isomorphism~\cite{xiao2024contrarc} received the Best Paper Award at DATE 2024. Our timing contracts enabled consistency and availability analysis of distributed systems~\cite{xiao2026memocode}. ACIES~\cite{xiao2026acies} and our ongoing work apply agent-proposed contracts to multi-robot systems and \gls{rtl} verification. The same discipline applies to multi-agent LLM and vision-language-action systems, in which a parent agent decomposes a task and assigns contracts to sub-agents.
