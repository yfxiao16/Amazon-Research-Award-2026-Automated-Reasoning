\section{Introduction}

Next-generation computing systems, from custom silicon to fleets of autonomous robots and workflows of cooperating AI agents, are increasingly designed and verified with the help of \gls{llm} agents. LLMs generate assertions and invariants for hardware~\cite{orenes2021autosva,kang2024fveval} and programs~\cite{wu2024lemur}, while the decomposition of the problem, where most of the difficulty lies, rests on the agent. End-to-end properties of large systems, such as in-order data delivery across a chain of hardware modules or mutual exclusion over a fleet of robots, often remain undetermined when checked on the flat design. In our experience, agents that decompose such properties without supervision may report timed-out runs as proofs or rely on circular assumptions across lemmas. Agentic software faces the same problem in another form: when an agent delegates subtasks or compacts its context, the rules it must follow are restated in natural language and may be lost~\cite{cemri2025mast}.

Contract-based design offers a principled foundation for decomposition in all these domains. An \gls{ag} contract specifies what a component guarantees under assumptions on its environment~\cite{benveniste2018contracts}. A decomposition is correct when the composition of the component contracts refines the system contract, and the algebra of contracts provides operators, such as the quotient, that compute the weakest specification a missing component must satisfy~\cite{incer2023algebraic,incer2022thesis}. Existing contract-based tools, including \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}, Pacti~\cite{incer2023pacti}, and OCRA~\cite{cimatti2013ocra}, assume that contracts are written by human designers, and existing decomposition methods for synthesis rely on a fixed library of components~\cite{iannopollo2018decomposition}. Letting agents write the contracts raises several scientific questions. The existence of a decomposition into realizable components is undecidable for general architectures~\cite{pnueli1990distributed}, so an agent may search indefinitely unless each failure is explained in a form that excludes whole families of candidates. The contract operators must also remain computable within the fragments that synthesis tools accept; for instance, the guarantee of the quotient of two GR(1) contracts contains the negation of assumptions and in general falls outside the GR(1) fragment.

In this project, we propose a framework, termed \sysname, aiming to address these challenges by following a three-pronged approach that builds on our prior work. \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase} provides an extensible contract algebra and interfaces to verification and synthesis tools, and has recently been extended with an interface for LLM agents. Our work on contract-based architecture exploration~\cite{xiao2024contrarc,xiao2026smcp} has shown that infeasibility certificates can be generalized via subgraph isomorphism to prune large design spaces, and received the Best Paper Award at DATE 2024. ACIES~\cite{xiao2026acies} uses LLM agents to decompose multi-robot requirements into GR(1) contracts that are certified and synthesized into controllers, and our ongoing work applies the same principle to \gls{rtl} verification. If successful, this project will lay the foundations for agentic design flows in which every decomposition proposed by an AI agent, for a hardware property, a robot mission, or a software task, comes with a certificate that a solver can check, and in which certified components are reused across designs.
