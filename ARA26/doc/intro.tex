\section{Introduction}

\Gls{llm} agents are increasingly employed to design complex systems, from custom silicon to controllers for fleets of autonomous robots and workflows of cooperating software agents. Agents generate hardware assertions~\cite{orenes2021autosva,kang2024fveval}, program invariants~\cite{wu2024lemur}, and code at a scale no design team can review. However, AI-generated designs are error-prone, and the errors concentrate in decomposition, which every complex design requires. In our experience, agents that decompose end-to-end hardware properties may report timed-out runs as proofs or rely on circular assumptions across lemmas. Agents that delegate subtasks or compact their context restate the rules they must follow in natural language and drift away from them as the task proceeds~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Moreover, the reasoning behind a design is buried in natural-language transcripts, so an error found late cannot be traced back to the decision that caused it. As a result, AI-driven design of complex systems relies today on trial and error and on testing the final artifact. A new generation of design methods would be essential to make every step of AI-driven design traceable and checkable, and to keep the overall design correct as it is decomposed across components, agents, and abstraction levels.

Contract-based design offers a principled foundation for this goal. An \gls{ag} contract specifies what a component guarantees under assumptions on its environment~\cite{benveniste2018contracts}. A decomposition is correct when the composition of the component contracts refines the system contract, and the algebra of contracts provides operators, such as the quotient, that compute the weakest specification a missing component must satisfy~\cite{incer2023algebraic,incer2022thesis}. Contracts also give each step of a design a formal record that can be checked, stored, and revisited. Existing contract-based tools, including \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase}, Pacti~\cite{incer2023pacti}, and OCRA~\cite{cimatti2013ocra}, assume that contracts are written by human designers, and existing decomposition methods for synthesis rely on a fixed library of components~\cite{iannopollo2018decomposition}. Letting agents write the contracts raises several scientific questions. The existence of a decomposition into realizable components is undecidable for general architectures~\cite{pnueli1990distributed}, so an agent may search indefinitely unless each failure is explained in a form that excludes whole families of candidates. The contract operators must also remain computable within the fragments that synthesis tools accept; for instance, the guarantee of the quotient of two GR(1) contracts contains the negation of assumptions and in general falls outside the GR(1) fragment.

In this project, we propose a framework, termed \sysname, aiming to address these challenges by following a three-pronged approach that builds on our prior work. \chase~\cite{nuzzo2018chase} provides a contract algebra with interfaces to verification and synthesis tools and to LLM agents. Our work on contract-based architecture exploration~\cite{xiao2024contrarc,xiao2026smcp} has shown that infeasibility certificates can be generalized via subgraph isomorphism to prune large design spaces, and received the Best Paper Award at DATE 2024. ACIES~\cite{xiao2026acies} uses LLM agents to decompose multi-robot requirements into GR(1) contracts that are certified and synthesized into controllers, and our ongoing work applies the same principle to \gls{rtl} verification. If successful, this project will lay the foundations for AI-driven design of complex systems in which every decomposition proposed by an agent, for a hardware property, a robot mission, or a software task, is recorded as a set of contracts with a certificate that a solver can check, errors are traced to the step that caused them, and certified components are reused across designs.
